Email Detective: spot the scam

Students learn to recognize a scam email (phishing), to find out where a link really goes, and to know what to do when a suspicious message arrives or when someone falls for one.

Gradeages 9–15
Length45 minutes
Subjectcomputing, form time
Formatwhole class, pairs

Learning goals

By the end of the lesson, students can:

  • name at least three signs of a scam message (rushing and scaring, a prize, a request for a password or details, a strange link, an unexpected attachment),
  • find the real domain in a web address (right before the first slash),
  • decide whether a message is safe or a scam and explain why,
  • respond correctly: don’t click, check another way, report it and tell an adult.

Materials

  • Projector and teacher computer. Student computers or tablets (one per pair is enough).
  • The Password Quest app: the Email Detective menu.
  • Worksheet (page 3), one per student.
Important: all messages, names and addresses in these materials and in the app are made up (.example and .test domains). Do not open real scam emails or links in class.

Lesson plan

Signs of a scam email (write on the board):
  1. Rushing and scaring: “We will block your account within an hour!”
  2. A tempting prize or gift for a click or your details.
  3. A request for a password, a text-message code or card details. Schools and trustworthy services never ask for these in a message.
  4. The link goes to a strange domain, even if its text looks right.
  5. An unexpected attachment, especially a program (.exe) or a document that wants you to “enable macros”.
  6. A request for secrecy: “Don’t tell anyone about this.”

Adapting for age

Younger students (ages 9–10)

  • Skip task 2 (addresses) or do only the first two rows together.
  • Talk about “bait”: the message wants to hook us like a fish.
  • Main rule: if a message wants your password or promises a prize, tell an adult.

Older students (ages 13–15)

  • Add advanced tricks: look-alike letters (schooI.example), the @ sign in an address, QR codes.
  • Discuss why scam messages no longer have spelling mistakes (artificial intelligence).
  • Mission Responding to phishing: what to do when a classmate entered their password on a fake site.

Things to watch out for

  • Teach students to tell the difference, not to be afraid. Not every email is a scam; every inbox in the app also contains real messages.
  • If a student says they already entered a password somewhere, help them change it right away and inform the administrator according to school rules, without shaming.
  • If your school has a “Report phishing” button or an administrator’s email, show students where to find it.

For the teacher · Sample lesson 2

Worksheet answers

1. Suspicious signs (7)

  • The sender address super-gifts.test belongs to neither the school nor a known company.
  • The subject and text promise a prize (bait).
  • Rushing: “within 30 minutes”.
  • A request for a username and password.
  • The link text looks like the school’s, but the link goes to the domain win-tablet.test.
  • A request for secrecy: “Don’t tell anyone about the prize”.
  • The attachment .pdf.exe is a program, not a document.

The greeting “Hi” is not suspicious on its own. If students circle it, explain that what matters is what the message asks for.
