A strong password from three words

Students find out why some passwords are easy to guess, build a made-up password from three random words in the Password Workshop, and learn why every account needs a different password.

Gradeages 8–15
Length45 minutes
Subjectcomputing, form time
Formatwhole class, pairs

Learning goals

By the end of the lesson, students can:

  • explain why short, predictable passwords (123456, a name and a year, a word with a number) are weak,
  • create a made-up password from three words that have nothing in common,
  • explain why the same password should not be used for several accounts,
  • say who to turn to when they have a problem with a password or an account.

Materials

  • Projector and teacher computer. Student computers or tablets (one per pair is enough).
  • The Password Quest app: the Passwords menu (missions) and the Password Workshop.
  • Worksheet (page 3), one per student. Scissors if you want to cut out the word cards.
Key rule for the lesson: students never write their real passwords, not in the app and not on paper. Every password in the lesson is a made-up example. Do not judge students by the passwords they use at home.

Lesson plan

TimeActivityWhat the teacher and students do
5 minWarm-up: a house keyAsk the class: “What would happen if one key opened your home, the school and your bike lock, and you lost it?” Link it to passwords: a password is the key to an account.
8 minVote: which password can I guess?Write on the board: 123456, Teddy123, Football2026, ElephantUmbrellaRocket. Students raise hands to vote for the easiest and hardest to guess. Name the patterns together: numbers in order, a name and numbers, a favorite thing and a year. Why is the last one strong? Three words that have nothing in common.
10 minPassword Workshop (pairs)Students open the Password Workshop, draw three word cards and look at the strength meter. Then they try three words of their own. On the worksheet (task 2) they write two made-up passwords and the workshop’s rating. Remind them: the words must not belong together (TableChairLamp is a poor choice).
12 minMissions in the appStudents play missions in the Passwords menu. Suggested level: ages 8–10 Digital Explorers (easy), ages 10–12 Account Guardians (medium), ages 12–15 Cyber Detectives (hard). Fast finishers continue with the next mission. Walk around and ask: “Why is this answer better?”
6 minOne key for every doorShort discussion: a game announces a password leak. What happens if I use the same password for my school email? Older students can try the simulation in the mission One key for every door (Account Guardians). Takeaway: a different password for every account, with help from a password manager approved by the school or parents.
4 minWrap-up and exit ticketRepeat the three rules together (box below). Students complete task 4 on the worksheet and hand it in on the way out.
Three rules to remember:
  1. Build a password from three words that have nothing in common, each starting with a capital letter.
  2. Every account gets a different password. No names, birth years or numbers in order.
  3. Never tell anyone your password. If you have a problem, tell a parent or a teacher.

Adapting for age

Younger students (ages 8–10)

  • Do task 2 with cut-out cards (page 3) first, then in the app.
  • Talk about the “secret key” of Bot the robot, who guards a digital castle.
  • Skip the leak simulation, a conversation about one key for every door is enough.

Older students (ages 12–15)

  • Add multi-factor authentication (mission MFA: the second factor).
  • Discuss why forcing a password change every week leads to patterns like Password1, Password2.
  • Explain what a password manager and its main password are for.

Things to watch out for

  • Students might think passwords from the game are fine to use for real. Stress that examples from the lesson must not be used for real; real passwords should be made up at home with a parent.
  • If a student says they shared a password or someone got into their account, respond calmly and without shaming. Help them change the password and inform a parent or the administrator according to school rules.
  • Student progress in the app is stored only in the browser on that device. The app collects no names or passwords.

For the teacher · Sample lesson 1

Worksheet answers

1. Sort the passwords

PasswordRatingWhy
123456ENumbers in order, the most common password of all.
Teddy123EA common word and numbers in order, a well-known pattern.
ZebraTulipBridgeSThree words that have nothing in common.
Football2026EA favorite thing and a year, a very common pattern.
CactusLampPillowSThree unrelated words, good length.

2. Build a password

Accept any password made of three (or more) unrelated words that the workshop rates Strong or Excellent. If a student uses related words (for example DogCatMouse), ask whether someone could guess them.

3. Complete the sentences

  • a) different / unique password
  • b) a friend (not even a best friend or a brother or sister)
  • c) a parent or a teacher (an adult I trust)
  • d) name or the year I was born

4. Exit ticket (examples of good tips)

  • Don’t use your name and birth year, they are easy to guess.
  • Build the password from three words that have nothing in common.
  • Use a different password for every account, otherwise one leak puts them all at risk.
  • Never tell anyone your password.
Follow-up activities: homework with parents, “Let’s check together whether our family uses the same password in several places” (without writing passwords down); next lesson: Email Detective (sample lesson 2).
